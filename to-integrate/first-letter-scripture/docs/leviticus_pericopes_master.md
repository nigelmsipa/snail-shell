# Leviticus Mnemonic Pericopes (LMP) - Complete Documentation

## Book Overview: Vayikra - "And He Called"

**Hebrew Title:** וַיִּקְרָא (Vayikra, "And He Called")  
**Purpose:** Manual for holy living in God's presence after redemption  
**Central Theme:** "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (19:2; 20:7, 26)  
**Structure:** Chiastic arrangement with Nadab/Abihu at center (ch. 10)

**Major Divisions:**
- **Part I: Establishing Atonement (Chapters 1-16)** - How sinners approach a holy God
- **Part II: Living in Holiness (Chapters 17-27)** - How the atoned community reflects God's character

**Key Concepts:**
- **Kedoshim** (קְדֹשִׁים) - "Holy ones" = set apart, distinct from common/unclean
- **Atonement** (כָּפַר, kaphar) - covering/cleansing of sin through blood
- **Blood** (דָּם, dam) - "The life of the flesh is in the blood" (17:11)
- **Offerings** - Five major types forming sacrificial pairs

**Typology:** The entire sacrificial system prefigures Christ's perfect sacrifice and ongoing high priestly ministry in the heavenly sanctuary (Hebrews 7-10).

## Part I: Establishing Atonement (Leviticus 1-16)

### Chapter 1: The Burnt Offering

#### 1:1-9 — Ascending Fire: The Cattle Offering
**Lore:** "The LORD called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting, saying..." (1:1). God initiates—He calls (וַיִּקְרָא, vayikra) Moses to receive instructions. The burnt offering (עֹלָה, olah, from עָלָה, alah, "to go up, ascend") represents complete surrender—the entire animal ascends in smoke to God. Unlike other offerings where portions are eaten, the burnt offering is wholly consumed by fire, symbolizing total dedication of one's life to God. The first type: cattle (1:3-9). Must be male without blemish (1:3)—prefiguring Christ, the spotless Lamb. The worshiper brings it to the tent entrance, lays hands on the animal's head (1:4), identifying with the substitute. "And he shall kill the bull before the LORD, and Aaron's sons the priests shall bring the blood and throw the blood against the sides of the altar" (1:5). The ritual: slaughter at tent entrance, priests sprinkle blood around the altar, cut animal into pieces, arrange on altar wood over fire (1:6-8). Entrails and legs washed with water (1:9). "And the priest shall burn all of it on the altar, as a burnt offering, a food offering with a pleasing aroma (רֵיחַ נִיחוֹחַ, reiach nichoach, lit. "aroma of rest/satisfaction") to the LORD" (1:9). The "pleasing aroma" depicts God's acceptance, not that He needs food. Typology: Ephesians 5:2, "Christ loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God."
*Verses: 9 | Theme: Total consecration*

#### 1:10-17 — Small Livestock and Birds
**Lore:** Second type: sheep or goats (1:10-13). Same requirements: male without blemish. "He shall kill it on the north side of the altar before the LORD, and Aaron's sons the priests shall throw its blood against the sides of the altar" (1:11). Cut into pieces, priest arranges on altar, entrails and legs washed, burn all (1:12-13). "A pleasing aroma to the LORD" (1:13). Third type: birds for the poor (1:14-17)—turtledoves or pigeons. The priest brings it to the altar, wrings off its head, drains blood on the side of the altar (1:15). Remove crop and feathers, throw aside (1:16). Tear it open by wings without dividing it completely, burn on altar (1:17). God's grace accommodates poverty—even the poorest can offer burnt offerings. Peter Lee notes the burnt offering pairs with the grain offering to represent "all of life"—animal and plant. The rich imagery of ascending smoke hints at Christ's obedience "pleasuring" the Father. SDA perspective: The continual burnt offering (morning and evening, Exodus 29:38-42) foreshadows Christ's perpetual intercession in the heavenly sanctuary.
*Verses: 8 | Theme: Accessible worship*

### Chapter 2: The Grain Offering

#### 2:1-10 — Fine Flour and Baked Offerings
**Lore:** "When anyone brings a grain offering (מִנְחָה, minchah, "gift, tribute") as an offering to the LORD, his offering shall be of fine flour" (2:1). Unlike the animal sacrifices, the grain (or meal) offering consists of flour, oil, and frankincense—produce of human labor and the earth's fruit. This offering emphasizes devotion from human work. The worshiper brings finely ground flour (the best quality), mixes it with oil, and adds frankincense (2:1). A portion is burned as a memorial offering (אַזְכָּרָה, azkarah, "memorial portion") on the altar; the rest goes to the priests (2:2-3). "It is a most holy part of the LORD's food offerings" (2:3). Alternate forms: baked cakes in an oven (2:4), griddle cakes (2:5-6), pan-fried cakes (2:7-10). Each form prepared with oil. "And you shall bring the grain offering that is made of these things to the LORD, and when it is presented to the priest, he shall bring it to the altar" (2:8). Peter Lee emphasizes that burnt (animal life) and grain (plant life) offerings together "represent all of life," uniting human industry with creature life.
*Verses: 10 | Theme: Labor offerings*

#### 2:11-16 — No Leaven, Salt Required, and Firstfruits
**Lore:** Two absolutes: (1) "No grain offering that you bring to the LORD shall be made with leaven, for you shall burn no leaven nor any honey as a food offering to the LORD" (2:11). Leaven symbolizes sin and corruption (1 Corinthians 5:6-8); honey ferments. The grain offering must be pure. You may bring them as firstfruits, but not on the altar (2:12). (2) "You shall season all your grain offerings with salt. You shall not let the salt of the covenant with your God be missing from your grain offering; with all your offerings you shall offer salt" (2:13). Salt preserves and purifies, symbolizing the eternal covenant. Jesus: "You are the salt of the earth" (Matthew 5:13). Firstfruits: "If you offer a grain offering of firstfruits to the LORD, you shall offer for the grain offering of your firstfruits fresh ears, roasted with fire, crushed new grain" (2:14). Mix with oil and frankincense (2:15). The priest burns memorial portion (2:16)—acknowledging God as the source of all provision. Typology: Christ is the "bread of life" (John 6:35). The grain offering, ground and processed through fire, prefigures Christ's suffering. The oil represents the Holy Spirit's anointing; the frankincense, His fragrant prayers.
*Verses: 6 | Theme: Purity and covenant salt*

### Chapter 3: The Peace Offering

#### 3:1-17 — Shared Feast: The Peace Offering
**Lore:** "If his offering is a sacrifice of peace offering (זֶבַח שְׁלָמִים, zevach shelamim, "sacrifice of peace/wholeness"), if he offers an animal from the herd, male or female, he shall offer it without blemish before the LORD" (3:1). The peace (or fellowship) offering is unique: only the fat and internal organs are burned on the altar (3:3-5, 9-11, 14-16); the rest is shared between the priests and the worshiper in a communal meal. This offering depicts fellowship—a sacred banquet "with God." Unlike the mandatory sin offerings, the peace offering is voluntary, expressing gratitude, fulfilling a vow, or celebrating God's blessings. Three animals qualify: cattle (3:1-5), sheep (3:6-11), or goats (3:12-16). Gender doesn't matter (male or female acceptable), symbolizing inclusivity in fellowship. The ritual: lay hands on animal, slaughter at tent entrance, priests sprinkle blood on altar, burn fat portions (3:2-5). "All fat is the LORD's" (3:16)—a perpetual statute. Fat represents the best, richest portion; it belongs to God alone. Eating fat or blood is strictly forbidden (3:17). Peter Lee observes that after atonement is accomplished by other sacrifices, "the peace offering can be offered—by which this holy person can now share fellowship with the Lord" in a meal. The joyful communal aspect contrasts with the solemnity of sin offerings. Exodus 24:11 depicts Moses, Aaron, and elders eating and drinking in God's presence after the covenant ratification—a peace offering moment. Typology: The peace offering foreshadows the Lord's Supper—communion with Christ. Believers feast with God through Christ, our peace (Ephesians 2:14). The shared meal anticipates the Marriage Supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9). SDA perspective: The peace offering reflects the restored fellowship between God and humanity after sin is atoned—living in shalom (wholeness, peace) with the Creator.
*Verses: 17 | Theme: Fellowship with God*

### Chapter 4-5: The Sin Offering

#### 4:1-35 — Sin's Remedy: The Purification Offering
**Lore:** "And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, 'Speak to the people of Israel, saying, If anyone sins unintentionally (בִּשְׁגָגָה, bishgagah, "in error, inadvertently") in any of the LORD's commandments about things not to be done, and does any one of them...'" (4:1-2). The sin offering (חַטָּאת, chattat, "sin" or "purification offering") atones for unintentional sins that defile holy things. Four categories of offenders, each with corresponding rituals: (1) **Anointed priest** (4:3-12): If the high priest sins, the entire community is implicated. He brings a bull, lays hands on its head, slaughters it, sprinkles blood seven times before the veil, applies blood to altar horns, pours remaining blood at altar base (4:5-7). The bull is burned outside the camp—not eaten (4:11-12). (2) **Whole congregation** (4:13-21): If the community sins unknowingly, elders lay hands on a bull, same blood ritual as priest's offering. (3) **Leader** (4:22-26): A male goat. Blood applied to bronze altar horns (not veil), fat burned, priest eats the remainder. (4) **Common person** (4:27-35): Female goat or lamb. Same altar ritual; priest eats. The key phrase: "And the priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven" (4:26, 31, 35). Once blood is applied, forgiveness is granted—a pattern applied to Christ's perfect blood (Hebrews 9:7, 22). The sin offering cleanses ritual impurity caused by sin. Peter Lee notes sin and guilt offerings "deal with different aspects of sin—impurity and debt." Sin offerings address defilement; guilt offerings address restitution. Typology: 2 Corinthians 5:21, "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God." Christ is our sin offering, bearing our guilt outside the camp (Hebrews 13:11-13). The blood sprinkled before the veil prefigures Christ's blood in the heavenly sanctuary (Hebrews 9:12).
*Verses: 35 | Theme: Atonement for unintentional sin*

#### 5:1-13 — Specific Sins Requiring Atonement
**Lore:** Chapter 5:1-13 details specific scenarios requiring a sin offering: withholding testimony (5:1), touching unclean things (5:2-3), rash oaths (5:4). When guilt is realized, confession is required: "he shall confess the sin he has committed" (5:5). The offering varies by economic status: lamb or goat (5:6), two turtledoves or pigeons for the poor (5:7-10), fine flour for the very poor (5:11-13). God's grace accommodates poverty—even the poorest can receive atonement. The flour offering (no oil or frankincense, since it's for sin) is partially burned; the priest eats the rest (5:12-13). "Thus the priest shall make atonement for him for the sin which he has committed in any one of these things, and he shall be forgiven" (5:13). Atonement + forgiveness formula repeated. This sliding scale of offerings shows God's concern for justice and mercy—no one is excluded from atonement due to poverty.
*Verses: 13 | Theme: Accessible atonement*

#### 5:14-6:7 — Restoring Grace: The Guilt Offering
**Lore:** The guilt offering (אָשָׁם, asham, "guilt, trespass") addresses sins involving misuse of holy things or wrongs done to others, requiring restitution. Two categories: (1) **Unintentional sacrilege** (5:14-19): Misusing sacred items (e.g., eating dedicated food unknowingly). Bring a ram without blemish, valued in silver shekels. Make restitution plus 20% (5:16). (2) **Interpersonal wrongs** (6:1-7): Deception, theft, false oaths regarding property. Restore the item plus 20%, then bring a ram as guilt offering (6:4-7). Peter Lee emphasizes guilt offerings "focus on payment of debt"—both to God (sacred violations) and neighbor (social violations). The restitution component distinguishes this from the sin offering. Together, sin and guilt offerings address the dual nature of transgression: moral corruption (impurity) and relational damage (debt). The 20% penalty ensures justice is served. Typology: Isaiah 53:10, "When his soul makes an offering for guilt (asham), he shall see his offspring." Christ is our guilt offering, satisfying both God's justice and restoring our relationship with Him and others. His death is full restitution for humanity's debt. SDA perspective: The sanctuary system teaches that sin has consequences requiring both cleansing (sin offering) and restitution (guilt offering). Christ's atonement addresses both dimensions—purification and reconciliation.
*Verses: 21 | Theme: Restitution and atonement*

### Chapter 6: Priestly Duties

#### 6:8-30 — The Perpetual Fire and Holy Things
**Lore:** These verses shift from the layperson's perspective to the priest's duties regarding sacrifices. God gives Moses detailed instructions for Aaron and his sons. **Burnt offering** (6:8-13): The fire on the altar must burn continually; it shall never go out (6:13). Each morning, the priest removes ashes, dresses in linen, and maintains the fire. The perpetual fire symbolizes God's constant presence and readiness to receive offerings. **Grain offering** (6:14-23): Regulations for preparing and consuming the grain offering. Priests eat the remainder in a holy place (6:16). Aaron's sons' ordination offering: fine flour, half in morning, half in evening, wholly burned (6:19-23). **Sin offering** (6:24-30): The sin offering is most holy. The priest who offers it shall eat it in a holy place (6:26). If blood is sprinkled on a garment, it must be washed in a holy place (6:27). If cooked in an earthen vessel, the vessel must be broken; if bronze, scoured and rinsed (6:28). Extreme care with holy things! But sin offerings whose blood is brought into the tent for atonement must not be eaten; they are burned outside the camp (6:30). Peter Lee notes these regulations stress that offerings are "the only means" Israel has to remain in God's presence. Priests' integrity and ritual exactness are crucial because they mediate atonement for the people.
*Verses: 23 | Theme: Perpetual fire and holy offerings*

### Chapter 7: Offerings Completed

#### 7:1-10 — The Guilt Offering and Priestly Portions
**Lore:** **Guilt offering** (7:1-10): Similar to sin offering. Most holy. Priest eats it; blood sprinkled on altar. The priest who offers burnt offering gets the hide (7:8). Every grain offering, whether mixed with oil or dry, belongs to the sons of Aaron equally (7:9-10). The phrase "the LORD spoke to Moses" recurs, underscoring Moses' authority in prescribing rituals. These priestly portions ensure the priesthood is sustained by the offerings—God provides for His servants through His people's worship.
*Verses: 10 | Theme: Priestly portions*

#### 7:11-21 — Peace Offering Regulations
**Lore:** Further laws for the peace offering. Three types: (1) **Thanksgiving** (7:12-15): Offered with unleavened cakes mixed with oil and leavened bread. One cake of each type is given to the priest; the rest eaten by the worshiper. Must be eaten the same day—nothing left till morning (7:15). (2) **Vow or freewill** (7:16-18): May be eaten on the day offered and the next day. Anything remaining on the third day must be burned. If eaten on the third day, the offering is not accepted; it becomes an abomination (פִּגּוּל, piggul, "foul, rotten"), and the person bears iniquity (7:18). Strict timeline ensures freshness and reverence. Unclean persons eating peace offerings are cut off from the people (7:19-21). Holiness and cleanliness are non-negotiable in worship.
*Verses: 11 | Theme: Thanksgiving and fellowship*

#### 7:22-38 — Prohibitions and Priestly Portions
**Lore:** Absolute prohibition: "You shall eat no fat of ox or sheep or goat" (7:23). Fat belongs to the LORD alone (3:16). Eating it results in being cut off (7:25). Equally absolute: "You shall not eat any blood, whether of fowl or of animal, in any of your dwelling places. Whoever eats any blood, that person shall be cut off from his people" (7:26-27). These laws enforce Israel's consecration. Life is in the blood (17:11); it is sacred, reserved for atonement. Peter Lee observes that because blood "gives life to all creatures," it is holy and forbidden for consumption. Christ later prohibits drinking blood (John 6:53) and redefines it as participation in His covenant. Peace offering portions: The worshiper brings the offering, but the breast is waved before the LORD and given to the priests; the right thigh is also the priest's portion (7:28-34). This is a perpetual due from the people of Israel (7:34). The chapter concludes: "This is the law of the burnt offering, of the grain offering, of the sin offering, of the guilt offering, of the ordination offering, and of the peace offering, which the LORD commanded Moses on Mount Sinai, on the day that he commanded the people of Israel to bring their offerings to the LORD, in the wilderness of Sinai" (7:37-38). Summary statement linking all offerings to Sinai's covenant. The sacrificial system is complete; now comes its implementation.
*Verses: 17 | Theme: Sacred prohibitions*

### Chapters 8-9: Priestly Consecration

#### 8:1-36 — Consecrating the Priests: Ordination
**Lore:** "The LORD spoke to Moses, saying, 'Take Aaron and his sons with him, and the garments and the anointing oil and the bull of the sin offering and the two rams and the basket of unleavened bread. And assemble all the congregation at the entrance of the tent of meeting'" (8:1-3). Moses obeys. The entire community witnesses the consecration—public ordination validates the priesthood. **Washing** (8:6): Aaron and sons washed with water—ritual purification. **Vesting** (8:7-9): Moses dresses Aaron in priestly garments: tunic, sash, robe, ephod, breastpiece with Urim and Thummim, turban with golden plate ("Holy to the LORD"). Each garment signifies holiness and mediation. **Anointing** (8:10-12): Moses anoints the tabernacle and all its furnishings with oil, consecrating them. He pours oil on Aaron's head, anointing him (8:12). The high priest is "anointed" (מָשִׁיחַ, mashiach, "messiah")—prefiguring the Messiah, the ultimate Anointed One. **Offerings** (8:13-29): Moses dresses Aaron's sons, then offers a sin offering (bull, 8:14-17), a burnt offering (ram, 8:18-21), and the ram of ordination (8:22-29). Blood from the ordination ram is applied to Aaron's and his sons' right ear, right thumb, right big toe (8:23-24)—sanctifying hearing, action, and walk. Moses sprinkles blood and anointing oil on their garments (8:30). **Seven-day consecration** (8:31-36): They remain at the tent entrance for seven days, eating the ordination offerings, not leaving lest they die (8:35). Moses: "This is what the LORD has commanded to be done" (8:36). Liturgically, this inaugurates the "kingdom of priests" (Exodus 19:6). Peter Lee notes the triadic structure: washing, anointing, offering, followed by divine approval (chapter 9). Typology: Hebrews 5:4-5, "No one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was. So also Christ did not exalt himself to be made a high priest." Christ is the ultimate High Priest, anointed by the Spirit (Luke 4:18), consecrated through His own blood (Hebrews 9:12).
*Verses: 36 | Theme: Priestly consecration*

#### 9:1-24 — The First Offerings and God's Glory
**Lore:** "On the eighth day Moses called Aaron and his sons and the elders of Israel" (9:1). Eighth day = new beginning after seven days of consecration. Moses commands Aaron to offer a sin offering (calf) and burnt offering (ram) for himself, then offerings for the people: sin offering (goat), burnt offering (calf and lamb), peace offering (ox and ram), grain offering (9:2-4). "For today the LORD will appear to you" (9:4). Anticipation! Aaron approaches the altar, slaughters the calf, applies blood, burns fat (9:8-11). Then burnt offering (9:12-14), people's offerings (9:15-21). Aaron lifts hands toward the people and blesses them (9:22). Moses and Aaron enter the tent, then come out and bless the people. "And the glory of the LORD appeared to all the people. And fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the burnt offering and the pieces of fat on the altar, and when all the people saw it, they shouted and fell on their faces" (9:23-24). Divine fire consumes the offerings—God's acceptance! The people respond with worship. This theophany validates the priesthood and sacrificial system. The fire will burn continually on the altar from this moment forward (6:13). Chiastic note: What begins holy worship ends with God's glory appearing. Leviticus 8-9 transitions Israel from wilderness wanderers into a covenant community ruled by priestly law. Typology: Christ's ascension and sending of the Holy Spirit (Acts 2) parallels this inaugural fire. God's acceptance of Christ's sacrifice is demonstrated by resurrection and the Spirit's outpouring.
*Verses: 24 | Theme: God's glory manifested*

### Chapter 10: Nadab and Abihu

#### 10:1-7 — Forbidden Fire: The Sacrilege of Nadab and Abihu
**Lore:** "Now Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, each took his censer and put fire in it and laid incense on it and offered unauthorized fire (אֵשׁ זָרָה, esh zarah, "strange fire") before the LORD, which he had not commanded them" (10:1). Immediately after the glory of God appears and divine fire consumes the offerings, Aaron's two oldest sons presume to offer their own fire—not taken from the altar. "And fire came out from before the LORD and consumed them, and they died before the LORD" (10:2). Instant judgment. Moses tells Aaron: "This is what the LORD has said: 'Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified'" (10:3). Aaron holds his peace—silent submission to God's holiness. Moses commands Aaron's cousins to carry the bodies outside the camp (10:4-5). Aaron and his remaining sons (Eleazar and Ithamar) are forbidden to mourn visibly—no unkempt hair, no torn garments—lest they die and wrath come on the congregation (10:6-7). The priesthood must continue; personal grief cannot compromise sacred service. This narrative is the only story in Leviticus chapters 1-25—its centrality underscores the book's main theme: the absolute holiness of God and strictness of covenant obedience. The unauthorized fire contrasts with the LORD's own consuming fire in 9:24. Peter Lee points out that the Hebrew tabernacle instructions form a chiasm around this event: sacrifice/ordination → Nadab/Abihu → laws/purity. The strange fire incident is the chiasm's center, pivoting the book from cultic instructions to community holiness. Why did they die? Theories: (1) They offered incense at the wrong time. (2) They used common fire instead of altar fire. (3) They were drunk (10:8-11 immediately follows with prohibition on alcohol). Regardless, the lesson is clear: Approaching God requires reverence, obedience, and prescribed means. Uzzah touching the Ark (2 Samuel 6:6-7) and Ananias/Sapphira lying to the Spirit (Acts 5:1-11) echo this principle. Typology: Christ alone approaches the Father as High Priest. Any attempt to approach God outside of Christ is "strange fire." Hebrews 12:28-29, "Let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire."
*Verses: 7 | Theme: God's holiness demands obedience*

#### 10:8-20 — Regulations After the Tragedy
**Lore:** God speaks directly to Aaron (rare in Leviticus—usually He speaks to Moses). "Drink no wine or strong drink, you or your sons with you, when you go into the tent of meeting, lest you die. It shall be a statute forever throughout your generations. You are to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (10:9-10). Sobriety and discernment are essential for priests. Did Nadab and Abihu drink before offering? Possibly. Moses then addresses Aaron and his sons: You must eat the grain offering and peace offering portions in a holy place (10:12-15). But Moses discovers they burned the sin offering instead of eating it (10:16). He's angry at Eleazar and Ithamar: "Why have you not eaten the sin offering in the place of the sanctuary, since it is a thing most holy?" (10:17). Aaron answers: "Behold, today they have offered their sin offering and their burnt offering before the LORD, and yet such things as these have happened to me! If I had eaten the sin offering today, would the LORD have approved?" (10:19). Aaron's defense: Given the tragedy, eating the sin offering would have been inappropriate. Moses accepts this (10:20). The passage shows priestly regulations aren't rigid legalism—context and heart matter. But the overarching command remains: distinguish holy from common, clean from unclean. This is the priest's fundamental duty.
*Verses: 13 | Theme: Distinguishing holy from common*
