# Exodus: The Mnemonic Pericope (EMP) Framework
## Complete Pericope Divisions with Rich Theological Lore

**Book Theme:** Deliverance, Covenant, and God's Dwelling with His People

**Hebrew Name:** שְׁמוֹת (Shemot, "Names") — God knows His people by name and redeems them  
**Greek Name:** Ἔξοδος (Exodus, "Going Out") — The journey from slavery to freedom

**Structure:** 40 chapters, ~25 major pericope units
- Part I: Deliverance (Exodus 1-18) — God rescues His people
- Part II: Covenant & Worship (Exodus 19-40) — God dwells with His people

---

## Part I: Deliverance (Exodus 1-18)

### Chapter 1: Israel in Bondage

#### 1:1-7 — The Names Remembered
**Lore:** "These are the names (שְׁמוֹת, shemot) of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob" (1:1). The book opens with genealogy—God remembers individuals, not just a mass. The twelve sons become seventy descendants (1:5), fulfilling God's promise to Abraham that his offspring would multiply (Gen 15:5). "But the people of Israel were fruitful and increased greatly; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong, so that the land was filled with them" (1:7). This explosive growth (echoing Gen 1:28, "be fruitful and multiply") sets up the Egyptian fear response. The Hebrew verb פָּרָה (parah, "fruitful") connects Israel's multiplication to creation blessing—they are unstoppable because God is with them. Joseph dies (Gen 50:26), ending the Genesis era, but God's covenant continues.
*Verses: 7 | Theme: God remembers His people*

#### 1:8-14 — A New King Who Did Not Know Joseph
**Lore:** "Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph" (1:8). Historical debate: Was this a native Egyptian dynasty replacing the Hyksos (Semitic rulers), or simply generational amnesia? Either way, the theological point is clear—human gratitude fades, but God's covenant does not. Pharaoh says, "Behold, the people of Israel are too many and too mighty for us" (1:9). Fear drives oppression. Egypt enslaves Israel with "ruthless" (בְּפָרֶךְ, befarekh) labor—crushing, brutal work (1:13-14). They build store cities: Pithom and Raamses (1:11), likely referring to Pi-Ramesses, capital of Ramesses II (13th century BC). Yet "the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied" (1:12). Israel's growth under persecution prefigures the early church (Acts 4:1-4). Oppression cannot thwart God's purposes.
*Verses: 7 | Theme: Slavery and divine irony*

#### 1:15-22 — Defiance of the Midwives
**Lore:** Pharaoh escalates: kill Hebrew baby boys at birth (1:15-16). He commands the midwives Shiphrah (שִׁפְרָה, "beauty") and Puah (פּוּעָה, "splendor")—their names suggest dignity, not servility. "But the midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded" (1:17). The Hebrew phrase יִרְאָה אֱלֹהִים (yir'ah Elohim, "fear God") means reverence for God outweighs fear of Pharaoh. They lie to Pharaoh, saying Hebrew women give birth before they arrive (1:19)—civil disobedience rooted in higher allegiance. "So God dealt well with the midwives. And the people multiplied and grew very strong" (1:20). God honors courage: He gives the midwives families (1:21). Pharaoh then commands all Egyptians to throw Hebrew boys into the Nile (1:22). The waters of death become the stage for deliverance—Moses will be saved from the river that was meant to destroy him. Typology: Hebrews 11:23 praises Moses' parents for their faith; the midwives' faith anticipates this theme.
*Verses: 8 | Theme: Fear God, not tyrants*

### Chapter 2: Birth of the Deliverer

#### 2:1-10 — Moses in the Ark
**Lore:** A Levite man and woman (later named Amram and Jochebed, 6:20) have a son. Seeing he is "fine" (טוֹב, tov, "good"—same word used in creation, Gen 1:4), they hide him for three months (2:2). When they can no longer hide him, Jochebed makes a basket (תֵּבַת, tebat, "ark"—same word as Noah's ark, Gen 6:14) of papyrus, coats it with bitumen and pitch, and places him among the reeds by the Nile (2:3). Parallel to Noah: both are placed in arks to be saved from waters of judgment. Pharaoh's daughter finds him, has compassion (2:5-6), and ironically, Moses' own mother is hired as his nurse (2:7-9). Pharaoh's daughter names him מֹשֶׁה (Mosheh, "Moses"), saying "I drew him out (מָשָׁה, mashah) of the water" (2:10). The name is a play on Hebrew and Egyptian (Egyptian mes means "son," as in Thutmose, "son of Thoth"). Moses, the deliverer, is himself delivered—raised in Pharaoh's house, yet Hebrew by blood. Stephen recounts this in Acts 7:20-22: "Moses was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, and he was mighty in his words and deeds." God's providence: Moses is trained in the very court he will later confront.
*Verses: 10 | Theme: The deliverer delivered*

#### 2:11-15 — Moses Flees to Midian
**Lore:** Moses grows up and "went out to his people and looked on their burdens" (2:11). He sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew. "He looked this way and that, and seeing no one, he struck down the Egyptian and hid him in the sand" (2:12). Moses acts in solidarity but also in violence—he is not yet ready to be the deliverer. The next day, he sees two Hebrews fighting. When he intervenes, they rebuke him: "Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you mean to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?" (2:14). His secret is out. Moses' failure here contrasts with his later calling—he cannot deliver Israel by human strength alone. Pharaoh seeks to kill Moses; he flees to Midian (2:15), east of Egypt in the Sinai Peninsula. Acts 7:25 says "he supposed that his brothers would understand that God was giving them salvation by his hand, but they did not understand." Moses must learn: deliverance comes by God's power, not human zeal.
*Verses: 5 | Theme: Premature action*

#### 2:16-22 — Moses Marries Zipporah
**Lore:** At a well in Midian (wells are meeting places in Scripture—Isaac, Jacob, Jesus), Moses defends the seven daughters of Reuel (also called Jethro, 3:1) from aggressive shepherds (2:16-17). Reuel invites Moses to stay; Moses marries Zipporah (צִפֹּרָה, "bird") (2:21). He names his first son Gershom (גֵּרְשֹׁם, "sojourner there"), saying "I have been a sojourner (גֵּר, ger) in a foreign land" (2:22). Moses identifies as an exile—neither Egyptian nor fully at home in Midian. This 40-year period (Acts 7:30) in the wilderness prepares Moses to lead Israel through the same terrain. He transforms from a prince to a shepherd, learning humility and dependence on God. Ellen White: "In the school of self-denial and hardship [Moses] was to learn patience, to temper his passions" (Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 247).
*Verses: 7 | Theme: From prince to shepherd*

#### 2:23-25 — God Remembers His Covenant
**Lore:** "During those many days the king of Egypt died, and the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help" (2:23). Their cry (שַׁוְעָה, shav'ah, "cry for help") rises to God. "And God heard their groaning, and God remembered (זָכַר, zakhar) his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. God saw the people of Israel—and God knew" (2:24-25). Four powerful verbs: heard, remembered, saw, knew (יָדַע, yada, "knew intimately"). This is not God belatedly noticing—He has always known. But now the appointed time for deliverance has come (Gen 15:13-16 prophesied 400 years). God's remembering is covenantal action—He is about to intervene. The stage is set for the burning bush.
*Verses: 3 | Theme: God hears and acts*

### Chapter 3: The Burning Bush

#### 3:1-6 — Holy Ground
**Lore:** Moses is tending Jethro's flock near Horeb (חֹרֵב, "the mountain of God," also called Sinai). "The angel of the LORD appeared to him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush" (3:2). The bush (סְנֶה, seneh, possibly related to "Sinai") is burning but not consumed—symbol of God's holiness and Israel's preservation under affliction. Moses turns aside to see. God calls, "Moses, Moses!" (repetition shows urgency). Moses replies, "Here I am" (הִנֵּנִי, hineni—the response of availability, echoing Abraham in Gen 22:1). God says, "Do not come near; take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground" (3:5). The ground is holy (קָדוֹשׁ, qadosh, "set apart") because of God's presence. Moses hides his face, "for he was afraid to look at God" (3:6). God identifies Himself: "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (3:6). This is the covenant God, linking past promises to present action. Jesus cites this verse (Matt 22:32) to prove the resurrection—the patriarchs are alive to God.
*Verses: 6 | Theme: The God who calls*

#### 3:7-10 — The Divine Commission
**Lore:** God says, "I have surely seen (רָאֹה רָאִיתִי, ra'oh ra'iti, emphatic form: "I have absolutely seen") the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry...I know their sufferings" (3:7). God's compassion drives action. "I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up out of that land to a good and broad land, a land flowing with milk and honey" (3:8). The Promised Land is described with abundance metaphors. God's plan: "I will send you to Pharaoh that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt" (3:10). Moses is commissioned not as a military leader but as God's spokesman. The deliverance is God's work; Moses is the instrument.
*Verses: 4 | Theme: I have come down to deliver*

#### 3:11-15 — The Name Revealed: I AM WHO I AM
**Lore:** Moses objects: "Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh?" (3:11). God answers, "I will be with you" (3:12)—divine presence, not human ability, is the assurance. Moses asks, "If I come to the people of Israel and say to them, 'The God of your fathers has sent me,' and they ask me, 'What is his name?' what shall I say to them?" (3:13). God replies, "I AM WHO I AM" (אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה, Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh). He continues, "Say this to the people of Israel: 'I AM (אֶהְיֶה, Ehyeh) has sent me to you'" (3:14). Then God gives the covenant name: "Say to the people of Israel, 'The LORD (יְהוָה, YHWH), the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you.' This is my name forever" (3:15). YHWH derives from the verb הָיָה (hayah, "to be")—God is the self-existent, eternal One. "I AM" means: I am present, I will be what I will be, I am the one who causes to be. Jesus echoes this in John 8:58: "Before Abraham was, I am" (ἐγώ εἰμι, ego eimi). The burning bush theophany reveals God's character: holy, compassionate, eternal, and faithful to His covenant. The name YHWH is used over 6,800 times in the OT—it is THE covenant name. Ellen White: "The name I AM implies an eternal presence; the past, present, and future are alike to God" (Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 307).
*Verses: 5 | Theme: I AM WHO I AM*

#### 3:16-22 — Instructions and Promises
**Lore:** God instructs Moses: gather Israel's elders, tell them "the LORD, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob, has appeared to me" (3:16). Announce deliverance to "a land flowing with milk and honey" (3:17). God predicts Pharaoh's resistance: "I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand" (3:19). But God will strike Egypt with "all the wonders" (3:20), and Pharaoh will let them go. Moreover, Israel will "plunder the Egyptians" (3:22)—receiving gold, silver, and clothing as back wages for their slavery. This sets up the Exodus as both liberation and vindication. God's foreknowledge: the entire drama is under His control.
*Verses: 7 | Theme: God's promise and plan*

### Chapter 4: Moses' Reluctance and Return

#### 4:1-9 — Signs and Wonders
**Lore:** Moses objects again: "But behold, they will not believe me or listen to my voice, for they will say, 'The LORD did not appear to you'" (4:1). God provides three signs: (1) The rod becomes a serpent (4:2-5)—Moses' staff, symbol of authority, transforms and is restored. Prefigures Moses' power over Egypt's serpent-god symbolism. (2) Moses' hand becomes leprous then healed (4:6-7)—God can afflict and restore. (3) Water from the Nile will turn to blood (4:9)—foreshadowing the first plague. These signs authenticate Moses as God's messenger. The signs are not magic tricks but demonstrations of YHWH's power over creation and disease.
*Verses: 9 | Theme: God equips His servants*

#### 4:10-17 — Aaron as Spokesman
**Lore:** Moses says, "Oh, my Lord, I am not eloquent...I am slow of speech and of tongue" (4:10). Whether Moses had a speech impediment or simply felt inadequate is unclear. God responds, "Who has made man's mouth?...Is it not I, the LORD? Now therefore go, and I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall speak" (4:11-12). God's power overcomes human weakness. But Moses persists: "Oh, my Lord, please send someone else" (4:13). God's anger kindles, yet He provides Aaron as spokesman: "He shall speak for you to the people, and he shall be your mouth, and you shall be as God to him" (4:16). Moses-Aaron partnership: Moses receives revelation; Aaron communicates it. This prefigures Christ (God's Word) and the Holy Spirit (who speaks Christ's words, John 16:13-14).
*Verses: 8 | Theme: God uses the reluctant*

#### 4:18-31 — Moses Returns to Egypt
**Lore:** Moses seeks Jethro's blessing, returns to Egypt with his wife and sons (4:18-20). On the way, "the LORD met him and sought to put him to death" (4:24). This cryptic incident involves Moses' failure to circumcise his son. Zipporah quickly circumcises their son with a flint knife, touches Moses' feet with the foreskin, and says, "Surely you are a bridegroom of blood to me!" (4:25). God relents. The passage emphasizes covenant obedience: Moses, called to lead God's covenant people, must first keep the covenant sign (circumcision, Gen 17:9-14) in his own household. Moses meets Aaron in the wilderness (4:27). They gather Israel's elders; Aaron speaks, Moses performs the signs. "And the people believed; and when they heard that the LORD had visited the people of Israel and that he had seen their affliction, they bowed their heads and worshiped" (4:31). Faith and worship precede deliverance.
*Verses: 14 | Theme: Covenant faithfulness begins at home*

### Chapter 5: Pharaoh's Hardened Heart

#### 5:1-9 — Let My People Go
**Lore:** Moses and Aaron go to Pharaoh with God's demand: "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel, 'Let my people go, that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness'" (5:1). Pharaoh's response: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice and let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, and moreover, I will not let Israel go" (5:2). This is not ignorance but defiance—Pharaoh rejects YHWH's authority. He asks, "Who is YHWH?" The entire Exodus narrative will answer that question through ten plagues. Pharaoh increases Israel's workload: "Let heavier work be laid on the men that they may labor at it and pay no regard to lying words" (5:9). He calls God's promise "lying words." The pattern of hardening begins—Pharaoh's free will and God's sovereignty intertwine throughout Exodus (see 4:21, 7:3, 9:12). The SDA Bible Commentary notes: "Pharaoh hardened his own heart, and God hardened it—both are true. God confirmed Pharaoh's choice."
*Verses: 9 | Theme: The tyrant's defiance*

#### 5:10-21 — Oppression Intensified
**Lore:** The taskmasters announce: "You shall no longer give the people straw to make bricks, as in the past; let them go and gather straw for themselves. But the number of bricks that they made in the past you shall impose on them" (5:10-11). Impossible quota—same output, more work. Israel's foremen are beaten when they fall short (5:14). They cry to Pharaoh; he refuses (5:15-18). Israel turns on Moses and Aaron: "The LORD look on you and judge, because you have made us stink in the sight of Pharaoh and his servants, and have put a sword in their hand to kill us" (5:21). Moses faces rejection from his own people—a pattern repeated throughout wilderness wanderings. Deliverance often gets worse before it gets better. This tests Moses' faith and prefigures Israel's constant grumbling.
*Verses: 12 | Theme: Deliverance delayed, faith tested*

#### 5:22-23 — Moses Questions God
**Lore:** Moses returns to the LORD: "O Lord, why have you done evil to this people? Why did you ever send me? For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name, he has done evil to this people, and you have not delivered your people at all" (5:22-23). Honest lament—Moses doesn't understand God's plan. This raw prayer shows Moses' humanity. God doesn't rebuke him; instead, He reassures him (chapter 6). The pattern: God allows His servants to wrestle with doubt before revealing His purposes. Compare Job, Jeremiah, Habakkuk—lament is biblical.
*Verses: 2 | Theme: Wrestling with God's timing*

### Chapter 6: God's Covenant Promises

#### 6:1-9 — I Am the LORD
**Lore:** God responds to Moses' complaint: "Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh; for with a strong hand he will send them out, and with a strong hand he will drive them out of his land" (6:1). Then God reveals Himself: "I am the LORD (יְהוָה). I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty (אֵל שַׁדַּי, El Shaddai), but by my name the LORD (יְהוָה, YHWH) I did not make myself known to them" (6:2-3). Scholars debate this verse: Did the patriarchs not know the name YHWH? Genesis uses it (e.g., Gen 4:26, 15:2). The answer: They knew the name but not its full meaning—covenant deliverance. YHWH is now revealed as the God who acts in history to redeem. God reaffirms the covenant: "I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God, and you shall know that I am the LORD your God, who has brought you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians" (6:7). Four "I will" promises (6:6-8): (1) bring you out, (2) deliver you, (3) redeem you, (4) take you as my people. These form the basis of the Passover Seder's four cups of wine. Israel's response? "They did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery" (6:9). Oppression crushes hope—yet God proceeds.
*Verses: 9 | Theme: YHWH revealed as Redeemer*

#### 6:10-13 — Moses' Second Commission
**Lore:** God commands Moses again: "Go in, tell Pharaoh king of Egypt to let the people of Israel go out of his land" (6:11). Moses objects: "Behold, the people of Israel have not listened to me. How then shall Pharaoh listen to me, for I am of uncircumcised lips?" (6:12). "Uncircumcised lips" (עֲרַל שְׂפָתַיִם, aral sefatayim) means "unfit, impure speech"—Moses feels inadequate. God doesn't answer the objection directly; He simply commands again (6:13). Sometimes God's call isn't about our ability but His authority.
*Verses: 4 | Theme: God's command overrides inadequacy*

#### 6:14-27 — Genealogy of Moses and Aaron
**Lore:** A brief genealogy establishes Moses and Aaron as Levites (6:14-25). Moses is 80, Aaron 83 (7:7) when they confront Pharaoh. This genealogy interrupts the narrative but serves a purpose: Moses and Aaron are legitimate covenant members, descendants of Levi, positioned to be priests and leaders. Verse 26-27 summarize: "These are the Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said, 'Bring out the people of Israel from the land of Egypt by their hosts.' It was they who spoke to Pharaoh king of Egypt about bringing out the people of Israel from Egypt, this Moses and this Aaron." Repetition emphasizes their identity and mission.
*Verses: 14 | Theme: Covenant lineage confirmed*

#### 6:28-30 — Prelude to the Plagues
**Lore:** The narrative resumes: "On the day when the LORD spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt, the LORD said to Moses, 'I am the LORD; tell Pharaoh king of Egypt all that I say to you'" (6:28-29). Moses repeats his objection: "I am of uncircumcised lips. How will Pharaoh listen to me?" (6:30). God's patience with Moses' fear is remarkable. The stage is set for the plagues—God's systematic demonstration of power.
*Verses: 3 | Theme: Persistent calling*

### Chapter 7: The Contest Begins

#### 7:1-7 — Aaron as Prophet
**Lore:** God says to Moses, "See, I have made you like God to Pharaoh, and your brother Aaron shall be your prophet" (7:1). Moses represents God; Aaron speaks Moses' words. "But I will harden Pharaoh's heart, and though I multiply my signs and wonders in the land of Egypt, Pharaoh will not listen to you" (7:3-4). God predicts the outcome before it happens—this is not about persuading Pharaoh but displaying God's glory. "The Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I stretch out my hand against Egypt and bring out the people of Israel from among them" (7:5). The plagues are revelation: YHWH is supreme over all creation and all false gods. Moses is 80; Aaron is 83 (7:7). Their advanced age shows God uses the unlikely.
*Verses: 7 | Theme: God hardens and reveals*

#### 7:8-13 — The Sign: Staff to Serpent
**Lore:** God tells Moses and Aaron to perform a sign before Pharaoh: throw down the staff, and it will become a serpent (תַּנִּין, tannin, "serpent" or "dragon") (7:9). Aaron does so. Pharaoh summons his magicians, who replicate the miracle "by their secret arts" (7:11)—possibly sleight of hand, demonic power, or natural tricks (cobra charming was known in Egypt). But "Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs" (7:12). God's power is superior. Yet "Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he would not listen to them, as the LORD had said" (7:13). First instance of explicit hardening. The serpent imagery is significant: Egypt's cobra (uraeus) was a symbol of Pharaoh's power. Aaron's staff-serpent defeating theirs symbolizes YHWH's dominance.
*Verses: 6 | Theme: God's power superior*

#### 7:14-25 — First Plague: Water to Blood
**Lore:** God tells Moses: "Pharaoh's heart is hardened; he refuses to let the people go" (7:14). Go to him in the morning by the Nile. Say, "The LORD, the God of the Hebrews, sent me to you, saying, 'Let my people go, that they may serve me in the wilderness.' But so far, you have not obeyed" (7:16). Then God commands: "By this you shall know that I am the LORD: behold, with the staff that is in my hand I will strike the water that is in the Nile, and it shall turn into blood" (7:17). Aaron strikes the Nile; all water in Egypt—rivers, canals, ponds, even water in vessels—turns to blood (7:19-20). Fish die, the river stinks, Egyptians cannot drink (7:21). The Nile, Egypt's lifeline and a deity (Hapi, god of the Nile), is judged. Ellen White: "The river whose waters they had feared would be the grave of Israel's sons was turned to blood" (reflecting Pharaoh's earlier decree, 1:22). The magicians replicate this (how?—perhaps using already-polluted water and dyes) (7:22). Pharaoh's heart remains hard. The plague lasts seven days (7:25). First plague theme: God controls nature; Egypt's gods are impotent.
*Verses: 12 | Theme: Judgment on the Nile*

### Chapter 8: Plagues Two, Three, and Four

#### 8:1-15 — Second Plague: Frogs
**Lore:** God tells Moses: "Go in to Pharaoh and say to him, 'Thus says the LORD, "Let my people go, that they may serve me. But if you refuse to let them go, behold, I will plague all your country with frogs"'" (8:1-2). Aaron stretches his hand over the waters; frogs (צְפַרְדֵּעַ, tsefarde'a) swarm the land—into houses, bedrooms, ovens, kneading bowls (8:3-6). The frog was sacred to Heqet, Egyptian goddess of fertility. God mocks Egypt's gods. The magicians replicate it (8:7), but they cannot remove the frogs—they can only add to the problem. Pharaoh begs Moses, "Plead with the LORD to take away the frogs...and I will let the people go" (8:8). Moses prays; God removes the frogs (8:12-13). They die in heaps, and "the land stank" (8:14). But when Pharaoh sees relief, "he hardened his heart and would not listen to them, as the LORD had said" (8:15). Pattern established: plague → Pharaoh relents → God removes → Pharaoh hardens. This cycle reveals Pharaoh's deep rebellion.
*Verses: 15 | Theme: God mocks false gods*

#### 8:16-19 — Third Plague: Gnats
**Lore:** Without warning to Pharaoh, God commands Aaron: "Stretch out your staff and strike the dust of the earth, so that it may become gnats (כִּנִּים, kinnim, possibly lice or gnats) in all the land of Egypt" (8:16). He does; gnats cover man and beast (8:17). The magicians try to replicate it but fail (8:18). They tell Pharaoh, "This is the finger of God" (8:19). Even Egypt's sorcerers recognize divine power—yet Pharaoh's heart remains hard. "Finger of God" (אֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים, etsba Elohim) is significant: later, the Ten Commandments are written "by the finger of God" (31:18; Deut 9:10). Jesus uses this phrase: "If it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). The plagues are God's creative/destructive power at work—the same power that will inscribe His law.
*Verses: 4 | Theme: The finger of God*

#### 8:20-32 — Fourth Plague: Flies
**Lore:** God tells Moses: "Rise up early in the morning and present yourself to Pharaoh, as he comes out to the water, and say to him, 'Thus says the LORD, "Let my people go"...Or else, if you will not let my people go, behold, I will send swarms of flies (עָרֹב, arov, "mixture" or "swarms"—possibly flies, beetles, or mixed insects) on you and your servants and your people, and into your houses"'" (8:20-21). Key shift: "But on that day I will set apart the land of Goshen, where my people dwell, so that no swarms of flies shall be there, that you may know that I am the LORD in the midst of the earth. Thus I will put a division (פְדוּת, fedut, "redemption, distinction") between my people and your people" (8:22-23). From now on, Israel is protected—God distinguishes between covenant people and pagans. Pharaoh relents, offering compromise: "Go, sacrifice to your God within the land" (8:25). Moses refuses: Israel must journey three days into the wilderness (8:27). Pharaoh agrees, then hardens again after relief (8:32). Theme: God protects His own; half-measures are rejected.
*Verses: 13 | Theme: God makes distinction*

### Chapter 9: Plagues Five, Six, and Seven

#### 9:1-7 — Fifth Plague: Livestock Disease
**Lore:** God commands Moses: "Go in to Pharaoh and say to him, 'Thus says the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, "Let my people go"...For if you refuse to let them go and still hold them, behold, the hand of the LORD will fall with a very severe plague upon your livestock that are in the field, the horses, the donkeys, the camels, the herds, and the flocks'" (9:1-3). Tomorrow this will happen (9:5). "And the LORD did this thing the next day, and all the livestock of the Egyptians died, but not one of the livestock of the people of Israel died" (9:6). Total distinction—Israel's animals untouched. Pharaoh investigates, confirms it, yet "the heart of Pharaoh was hardened, and he did not let the people go" (9:7). Economic devastation doesn't move him. Egypt's sacred animals (bulls for Apis, cows for Hathor) die—their gods are powerless.
*Verses: 7 | Theme: Judgment on livestock*

#### 9:8-12 — Sixth Plague: Boils
**Lore:** God tells Moses and Aaron: "Take handfuls of soot from the kiln, and let Moses throw them in the air in the sight of Pharaoh. It shall become fine dust over all the land of Egypt, and become boils (שְׁחִין, shechin, "boils" or "sores") breaking out in sores on man and beast throughout all the land of Egypt" (9:8-9). They do so; painful boils afflict Egyptians and animals (9:10). "And the magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils, for the boils came upon the magicians and upon all the Egyptians" (9:11). The magicians, previously able to mimic, are now victims. They disappear from the narrative—unable to function. "But the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh, and he did not listen to them, as the LORD had spoken to Moses" (9:12). First time the text says "the LORD hardened" rather than "Pharaoh hardened"—God confirms Pharaoh's choice. The boils evoke the curses of covenant disobedience (Deut 28:27, 35). Egypt, under judgment, experiences covenant curses meant for rebellious Israel—ironic reversal.
*Verses: 5 | Theme: God hardens the hardened*

#### 9:13-35 — Seventh Plague: Hail and Fire
**Lore:** God tells Moses: "Rise up early in the morning and present yourself before Pharaoh and say to him, 'Thus says the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, "Let my people go, that they may serve me. For this time I will send all my plagues on you yourself, and on your servants and your people, so that you may know that there is none like me in all the earth"'" (9:13-14). God could have destroyed Pharaoh already, "but for this purpose I have raised you up, to show you my power, so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth" (9:16). Paul quotes this in Romans 9:17 to illustrate God's sovereignty. God warns: Tomorrow, heavy hail (בָּרָד, barad) will fall, mixed with fire (9:18). "Now therefore send, get your livestock and all that you have in the field into safe shelter, for every man and beast that is in the field and is not brought home will die when the hail falls on them" (9:19). Some Egyptians fear God's word and shelter their animals; others ignore it (9:20-21). Moses stretches his staff; thunder, hail, and fire rain down—"such as had never been in all the land of Egypt since it became a nation" (9:24). Everything in the fields—man, beast, plant—is struck. "Only in the land of Goshen, where the people of Israel were, was there no hail" (9:26). Pharaoh summons Moses: "This time I have sinned; the LORD is in the right, and I and my people are in the wrong. Plead with the LORD, for there has been enough of God's thunder and hail. I will let you go, and you shall stay no longer" (9:27-28). Moses prays; the storm stops. "But when Pharaoh saw that the rain and the hail and the thunder had ceased, he sinned yet again and hardened his heart, he and his servants" (9:34). False repentance—Pharaoh confesses sin without true change. The hail destroys flax and barley (early crops) but not wheat (late crop) (9:31-32)—God's judgment is precise, leaving Egypt time to repent.
